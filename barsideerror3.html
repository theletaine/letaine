Barside Errors III

Betta fish weave their way between cherries that bob on the water’s surface. The shiny skins look black under the fish tank’s cyan lights. Rumbling vibrates through the bar car while a hunched man sits on a stool and completes his prep work. Octini, the aforementioned man, focuses on a naked orange in his hand. He kicks out a leg and peels pith off the flesh. White strands pile on the bar counter as veins unravel, juice beads on the skin, and the orange loses more and more detail until it’s just a ball of solid color. His sticky nails jab into the orange, and he’s rewarded with a particularly juicy spray. He wrinkles his nose and flexes his temporarily jaundiced fingers. The perfumery won’t linger all the way to the restroom, will it? He sniffs at his hand. 

It is a terribly lovely scent. Might do some good to scent up the bathroom for the customers. He gets up to touch toilet rolls, but the flick of a betta’s tail stalls him. He stares up at the fish with their gauzy tails and orange slice-shaped bodies and wonders if pulling apart their tails a strand at a time is possible. The preferred method would be to split the center then work up to the top. He wouldn’t do it; it’s speculation, a loose thought that slips out of his head from time to time. Yellow-tipped fingers gather the threads of pith, and he pulls the bin out from under his chair. He puts the handful in his mouth and chews. He drags the trash can with one foot as he heads behind the bar counter.

Octini wipes down the counter and dips his hand into the ice bucket. Fingertips stiffen; all seems to be working. He hangs up the hand towel and moves past the towering shelves of spirits and booze until he reaches the blackout curtains. He uses his right elbow to pull the cloth up. A landscape of yellow grass and gray skies rush past the train window. Plains stretch out, even if he turns his head left or right. He glances at his watch. His sleeve covers his wrist. He pulls up his sleeve to look at his watch. His wrist is empty. He fishes a fluorescent stack of yellow sticky notes from his pocket and peers at the top note. 

Open at eight in the morning.

Must be a rainy morning for it to be so gray. Still, there’s bound to be at least a couple day drinkers. He returns to his position behind the counter, where another bright yellow square greets him.

✓ Check your notebook. Tucked in sash or on your room’s counter.

This note is already checked off. Octini fishes out the journal of recipes he already had between his sash and stomach. He skims through the “Day’s Preparations” section and recollects that garnishes are stored in metal bowls or glass jars behind the counter. Octini, lifting the wooden partition between the bar and the seating area, wanders to the fish tank before pressing his fingers to the transparent wall. The glass is filled with betta fish, glowing from cyan and teal lights installed at the top. He tugs his long sleeves up his forearms to his biceps. Using a cord, he ties back the length of cloth so that his arms are bare. A watch sits on his left wrist. The short hand points to two. A curious fish swims near the smudged glass. He picks up the metal bowl left beside the fish tank and brings it back, then wipes the counter because it’s sticky to the touch. He looks at the pad of sticky notes again.

Open at eight in the morning.

Glancing around at the bar, he notes that the bottles are already organized. He checks off the note. The bar car’s door hisses and unlatches. The groans of the train grow louder until the door clanks shut. Octini sets down his hand towel, and his gaze drifts to the entrance. He doesn’t recognize the woman with the perfectly curled bob, so his lips spread into a cheery smile.

“How may I get you started?” he asks.

“A highball, darling. No, no, make that two.”

“Any garnish?”

“A twist and spritz of lemon.”

He jots the order on a sticky note and pastes it to his wrist, underneath the watch. He marks a curlicue next to “highball.” He flips through the notebook until he finds the recipes.

He sets two ice cubes, ones that were clustered in a wooden tub at room temperature, into a tall glass before pouring off whiskey and then some soda. All that remains is the garnish.

A can sits behind the bar counter, next to the rocks glasses and between a bottle of bitters and a jar of oranges. Black worms glint in the tin container. He pushes his tweezers through the mass of noodling tangles. He pulls on a long guy. It’s got the sleek elegance of a sea snake, or the twisty sleaze of a legless dragon. It curlicues up in the cold air.

He sets the worm on the glass’s rim and wrinkles his nose while squeezing a lemon wedge. On an accompanying plate, he sets a pristinely peeled orange. He serves the woman. Her face twists up.

“The orange is complimentary,” he says.

—

He ends up adding what a “twist” means to the opening glossary of his notebook and subtracting two highballs from her bill.
